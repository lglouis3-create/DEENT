# Narration scripts, Exam 3 eye figures (N1)

## Aqueous humor: inflow and outflow (ey-aqueous)

**What it explains.** Where aqueous humor is made (inflow), the two routes it drains by (outflow), and where each glaucoma drug class acts. Dr. Smith, glaucoma lectures of 11/13 and 11/17.

### Step 1 of 7

**Caption on screen:** The parts of the flow pathway, labelled as on slide 15; the colours in the key stay the same in every step (slides 14–15).

**Narration (127 words):** This figure comes from Dr. Smith's glaucoma lectures. It shows where aqueous humor, the liquid in the front of the eye, is made, how it drains, and where each glaucoma drug acts. This first step only names the parts, in an eye cut from front to back. The cornea is at the front, and the iris has the pupil in its centre, with the lens behind it. The ciliary body, with its ciliary processes, ciliary muscle and ciliary epithelium, lies behind the iris. The trabecular meshwork and Schlemm's canal sit at the angle where the iris meets the cornea and sclera. The three green boxes give the flow in order: posterior chamber, anterior chamber, trabecular meshwork. The colours in the key stay the same in every step.

**Sources:** Slides 14-15; 11/13 T@19333-@33000

### Step 2 of 7

**Caption on screen:** Inflow: the ciliary body makes aqueous humor all the time; it flows through the pupil into the anterior chamber (slides 14–15; 11/13 T@25530).

**Narration (117 words):** Inflow is the technical term for the production of aqueous humor. The ciliary epithelium of the ciliary body makes it, and Dr. Smith stressed that it is made all the time and never stops. The green path in the picture starts at the ciliary body and enters the posterior chamber, the space between the lens and the iris. The liquid flows through the opening there, out through the pupil, and into the anterior chamber, the open space in front of the iris. The three boxes repeat these stages. Because production never stops, the liquid must drain continuously. If it does not drain, pressure builds inside the eye, and that is glaucoma. The next step shows the drainage.

**Sources:** Slides 14-15; 11/13 T@25530-@28180, T@28180 (inflow is production)

### Step 3 of 7

**Caption on screen:** Outflow: most drains through the trabecular meshwork and Schlemm’s canal; some by the uveoscleral route (slides 14–16; 11/13 T@32688).

**Narration (127 words):** Outflow is the technical term for the drainage of aqueous humor out of the eye, and there are two routes. The main route is through the trabecular meshwork, a porous tissue at the angle. The liquid passes through it into Schlemm's canal, a small duct, which empties into blood vessels, so the liquid leaves the eye in the blood. This is the dashed arrow. The smaller route is the uveoscleral route, the dotted arrow. The uvea includes the ciliary body, its attachments to the lens, and the iris, and the sclera is the white wall of the eye. The liquid squeezes between the uvea and the sclera and spreads into those tissues, without using a canal. Dr. Smith teaches that most outflow goes through the trabecular meshwork.

**Sources:** Slides 14-16; 11/13 T@28606-@34200

### Step 4 of 7

**Caption on screen:** Slide 16 zooms on a ciliary process: β-blockers act on the β receptor, α2 agonists on the α2 receptor, CA inhibitors on the enzyme (slides 16, 18, 22, 26).

**Narration (125 words):** This step zooms in on one ciliary process, a part of the ciliary body, to show what the inflow drugs act on. The green dot is the beta receptor, the target of beta blockers. Dr. Smith explained that stimulating this receptor gave more aqueous humor and blocking it gave less, although the exact mechanism is not understood. The amber dot is the alpha two receptor, the target of alpha two agonists. The purple dot is the enzyme carbonic anhydrase, the target of carbonic anhydrase inhibitors. An alpha one receptor and the ciliary blood supply are also labelled, but this figure attaches no drug to them. The drawing also marks M three on the iris and on the trabecular meshwork, which matters in the sixth step.

**Sources:** Slides 16, 18, 22, 26; 11/13 T@43883-@44200, T@53959-@60121

### Step 5 of 7

**Caption on screen:** Drugs that decrease inflow act on the ciliary body: β-blockers, α2 agonists and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (slides 16, 18, 22, 26; 11/13 T@39934).

**Narration (115 words):** Three drug classes lower inflow, which means they decrease the production of aqueous humor. All three act on the ciliary body. Beta blockers block the beta receptor. Alpha two agonists act on the alpha two receptor. Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors block the enzyme carbonic anhydrase. In the picture the inflow path is highlighted and the drainage routes are dimmed. Dr. Smith said beta blockers reduce production, have little effect on outflow, and that how they work is not fully understood. Dr. Smith groups every glaucoma drug by whether it affects inflow or outflow, and these three classes are the inflow group. The one addition, a small effect of brimonidine on outflow, appears in the last step.

**Sources:** Slides 16, 18, 22, 26; 11/13 T@39700-@40600, T@43883-@44200

### Step 6 of 7

**Caption on screen:** Trabecular outflow up: M3 agonists and echothiophate contract the meshwork; netarsudil makes it less stiff (slides 32, 34, 41; 11/17 T@24556).

**Narration (115 words):** Two groups raise outflow through the main route, the trabecular meshwork and Schlemm's canal. M three agonists, such as carbachol and pilocarpine, stimulate the M three receptor. Echothiophate is an irreversible acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, so it raises acetylcholine, which stimulates the same receptor. Dr. Smith teaches that this contracts the sphincter muscle and the trabecular meshwork, opening spaces in the meshwork so that more liquid drains. Netarsudil is a Rho kinase inhibitor. Rho A may raise the tone and stiffness of the meshwork, so inhibiting it relaxes the tone, lowers fibrosis-related protein deposits, and changes the permeability of the cells lining Schlemm's canal. Dr. Smith said this is not a muscular relaxation. Either way, outflow rises.

**Sources:** Slides 32, 34, 41; 11/17 T@23400-@24556, T@24556-@36600

### Step 7 of 7

**Caption on screen:** Uveoscleral outflow rises: PG agonists relax the ciliary muscle (also some trabecular); brimonidine adds some (slides 22, 37; 11/17 T@31096).

**Narration (120 words):** The last group raises outflow by the smaller route, the uveoscleral route. Prostaglandin agonists, called P G agonists, stimulate the P G F two alpha receptor on the ciliary muscle. This relaxes the muscle, and Dr. Smith said the relaxed muscle moves away from the sclera, so the space between them opens and more liquid leaves by the uveoscleral route. They also raise trabecular outflow, to a lesser extent, and they do not change production. Brimonidine, an alpha two agonist, lowers production and also adds some uveoscleral outflow. Every class can now be placed. Beta blockers, alpha two agonists and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors lower inflow. M three agonists, echothiophate and netarsudil raise trabecular outflow. P G agonists raise uveoscleral outflow.

**Sources:** Slides 22, 37; 11/17 T@31096-@33000

Echothiophate, also red, is an irreversible acetylcholinesterase inhibitor. It raises acetylcholine, which stimulates the same receptors. Contraction of the sphincter muscle and the trabecular meshwork increases outflow. The magenta markers belong to netarsudil, a Rho kinase inhibitor. Its sites are the trabecular meshwork, where it lowers stiffness, and the cells lining Schlemm's canal, where it changes permeability. P G agonists stimulate it, which relaxes the ciliary muscle. Dr. Smith explained that the relaxed muscle moves away from the sclera, so more liquid flows out by the uveoscleral route. These drugs do not affect aqueous humor production, and they raise trabecular outflow only to a lesser extent. The second amber marker belongs to brimonidine, an alpha two agonist. Dr. Smith's drawing shows alpha two agonists binding to the uveoscleral tissue, and brimonidine gives some increase in uveoscleral outflow. So brimonidine appears twice: it lowers inflow at the ciliary body and adds a little outflow here.

**Sources:** Slides 22, 37; 11/17 T@31096-@33000

### Step 4 of 4

**Caption on screen:** All the classes on one eye, one colour each: three lower inflow, three raise outflow by the routes shown (slides 18, 22, 26, 32, 34, 37, 41).

**Narration (108 words):** The last step puts all the classes on one eye, one colour each. Three classes lower inflow, and all three act on the ciliary body: beta blockers, alpha two agonists and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors. Three classes raise outflow by the routes shown. The cholinergic drugs, meaning M three agonists and echothiophate, and netarsudil raise trabecular outflow. P G agonists raise uveoscleral outflow, with some help from brimonidine. Each site is drawn only where Dr. Smith's lecture marks it, so a class with no marked site is not placed. For any class, this figure gives the structure or molecule it acts on and whether inflow falls or outflow rises.

**Sources:** Slides 18, 22, 26, 32, 34, 37, 41; 11/13 T@39934; 11/17 T@24556, T@31096

## Open-angle vs angle-closure glaucoma (ey-angle)

**What it explains.** Normal drainage compared with open-angle and angle-closure glaucoma: what blocks drainage in each, how each starts and why angle closure is an emergency. Dr. Smith, 11/13.

### Step 1 of 3

**Caption on screen:** Normal: aqueous humor drains at the angle (circle), where the iris meets the cornea and sclera (slide 14 panel A; 11/13 T@26451).

**Narration (126 words):** This figure compares the two main types of glaucoma with a normal eye, from Dr. Smith's glaucoma lecture. Glaucoma is a progressive eye disease in which damage to the optic nerve can lead to vision loss. This first step shows normal drainage in an eye cut from front to back. The ciliary body makes aqueous humor, which flows through the pupil into the anterior chamber. It drains at the angle, marked by the dashed circles, where the iris meets the cornea and sclera, through the trabecular meshwork and Schlemm's canal. The smaller uveoscleral route is labelled too. Because the liquid is always made, it must always drain. The goal of treating glaucoma is to lower the intraocular pressure, I O P, to protect the optic nerve.

**Sources:** Slides 13-14; 11/13 T@19603-@20900, T@23500-@28180, T@26451

### Step 2 of 3

**Caption on screen:** Open angle: the angle stays open but the trabecular meshwork drains poorly; gradual and silent (slides 13, 14 panel B; 11/13 T@29351).

**Narration (116 words):** The second step shows primary open-angle glaucoma, called P O A G. The angle stays open. The red slash marks the problem: the trabecular meshwork does not drain effectively. Dr. Smith teaches that P O A G is related to how the liquid drains or, more correctly, does not drain. Because the liquid keeps being made, the pressure inside the eye can build up, and over time it can damage the optic nerve. The onset is usually gradual, with no symptoms until visual field loss occurs. It is common in the United States. The goal is the same as for a normal eye: decrease the I O P to delay or prevent progression of the disease.

**Sources:** Slides 13, 14 panel B; 11/13 T@19603-@20900, T@29351-@33000

### Step 3 of 3

**Caption on screen:** Angle closure: the iris bows forward and blocks the meshwork; the pressure rises fast and hurts (slides 13, 14 panel C; 11/13 T@28606).

**Narration (126 words):** The third step shows primary angle-closure glaucoma, called P A C G. Dr. Smith teaches that it is connected with the shape of the eye and how it sits in the eye socket. Here the iris bows forward and blocks the trabecular meshwork, so the angle is closed and the liquid cannot drain. The pressure rises very quickly, and the patient has significant pain. It is a medical emergency, and the I O P must be lowered quickly. Dr. Smith gave a blow to the eye with swelling, or a chemical exposure, as causes of a closed angle, and she described a laser that opens the closed angle as the treatment. Both types share one goal: lower the I O P to protect the optic nerve.

**Sources:** Slides 13, 14 panel C; 11/13 T@23400-@24500, T@28606-@29351

## Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors: eye and kidney (acidosis) (ey-cai)

**What it explains.** How carbonic anhydrase inhibitors lower aqueous humor production in the eye, and why oral ones can cause metabolic acidosis through the kidney. Dr. Smith, 11/13 and 11/17.

### Step 1 of 5

**Caption on screen:** In the ciliary epithelium, carbonic anhydrase makes H₂CO₃; its HCO₃⁻ leaves with Na⁺ and water follows: aqueous humor (slide 26; 11/17 T@5420).

**Narration (110 words):** This figure explains two things about carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, called C A inhibitors: how they lower pressure in the eye, and why the oral ones can cause metabolic acidosis. It comes from Dr. Smith's glaucoma lectures. The first step zooms into the ciliary epithelium, the cells of the ciliary body that make aqueous humor. Inside the cell, the enzyme carbonic anhydrase joins carbon dioxide and water to make carbonic acid. The carbonic acid separates on its own into a hydrogen ion and bicarbonate. Bicarbonate leaves the cell together with sodium and enters the posterior chamber. Water follows these solutes, and that is how aqueous humor is made, which is inflow.

**Sources:** Slide 26; 11/13 T@54881-@57500; 11/17 T@4800-@5900

### Step 2 of 5

**Caption on screen:** A CA inhibitor stops the enzyme: less HCO₃⁻ and Na⁺ leave, less water follows, so inflow falls (slides 26, 28; 11/13 T@54881).

**Narration (114 words):** In the second step a C A inhibitor blocks the enzyme, shown by the bar over carbonic anhydrase. The cell can no longer make carbonic acid from carbon dioxide and water, so less bicarbonate is formed, and fewer solutes are available to leave the cell. With fewer solutes entering the posterior chamber, less water follows, and aqueous humor production falls. This is why these drugs lower inflow in glaucoma. The box lists the four drugs: dorzolamide and brinzolamide are eye drops, and acetazolamide and methazolamide are taken by mouth. Dr. Smith said eye drops are used first for glaucoma, and an oral inhibitor is added later if a patient is refractory to other therapies.

**Sources:** Slides 26, 28; 11/13 T@54881-@60121; 11/17 T@4800-@5900

### Step 3 of 5

**Caption on screen:** Kidney: CA IV in the lumen and CA II in the cell make HCO₃⁻, which returns to the blood and buffers its pH (slide 27; 11/17 T@8731).

**Narration (124 words):** Now the figure moves to the kidney, because the acidosis side effect happens there. This is a proximal tubule cell, with the lumen, where urine flows, on the left, and the blood on the right. In the lumen, hydrogen ion and bicarbonate form carbonic acid, and carbonic anhydrase four on the apical membrane splits it into carbon dioxide and water, which enter the cell. In the cell, carbonic anhydrase two remakes carbonic acid, which separates into hydrogen ion and bicarbonate. N H E three sends the hydrogen ion out in exchange for sodium coming in, and N B C one sends bicarbonate and sodium to the blood. Dr. Smith said these transporter names are not needed. Bicarbonate returning to the blood buffers its pH.

**Sources:** Slide 27; 11/17 T@5900-@10000

### Step 4 of 5

**Caption on screen:** Oral acetazolamide blocks CA IV and CA II: no H⁺ for NHE3, so Na⁺ and water stay in the lumen (diuresis) (slides 27, 29; 11/17 T@14835).

**Narration (121 words):** In the fourth step oral acetazolamide blocks both carbonic anhydrase four in the lumen and carbonic anhydrase two in the cell, shown by the red bars. Dr. Smith focused on the enzyme in the cell. Without that enzyme, no carbonic acid is made in the cell, so no hydrogen ion is made to be sent out through N H E three. With no hydrogen ion leaving, sodium cannot come in by exchange, so sodium stays in the lumen, and water stays with it. The result is more urine, which is diuresis, and this is how these drugs work as diuretics. Dr. Smith said diuresis is not the goal in glaucoma, but increased urination is a side effect of the oral drugs.

**Sources:** Slides 27, 29; 11/17 T@10000-@16000, T@21000-@22700

### Step 5 of 5

**Caption on screen:** No HCO₃⁻ returns to the blood, so the pH falls: metabolic acidosis, the side effect to watch with oral CAIs (slide 29; 11/17 T@17682).

**Narration (115 words):** The last step shows the blood. Because no carbonic acid is formed in the cell, no bicarbonate is formed there either, so no bicarbonate returns to the blood. Bicarbonate in the blood acts as a buffer, so without it the blood pH falls. That is metabolic acidosis. Dr. Smith said it is a significant side effect that must be monitored, and that it happens mainly with oral use and to a much lesser extent with eye drops. Dr. Smith also said that solute movement in the eye is not quite the same as in the kidney. In the eye, blocking the enzyme lowers aqueous humor production. In the kidney, the same block explains the acidosis.

**Sources:** Slide 29; 11/17 T@6785, T@17682-@20300

## Allergic conjunctivitis: histamine and the drugs (ey-allergy)

**What it explains.** How an allergen on IgE makes a mast cell release histamine, and where H1 inverse agonists, mast cell stabilizers and alpha-2 agonists act. Dr. Smith, 11/17.

### Step 1 of 4

**Caption on screen:** A sensitized mast cell carries IgE on its surface; the allergen (antigen) binds that IgE (slide 48; 11/17 T@41462).

**Narration (127 words):** This figure explains allergic conjunctivitis, the allergy of the eye, and where each drug class acts. It comes from Dr. Smith's lecture on allergic conjunctivitis. On the left is a mast cell, which stores histamine in granules, the small circles inside it. After a first exposure to an antigen, the mast cell expresses I g E on its surface, shaped to look for that antigen. The Y shapes are the I g E, and the brown diamonds are the allergen, such as cedar pollen, binding to it. This binding stimulates degranulation, the release of histamine and other inflammatory mediators, which the next step shows. On the right is the eye tissue, the conjunctiva, with H one receptors on its edge, and a small blood vessel below it.

**Sources:** Slide 48; 11/17 T@40500-@42500

### Step 2 of 4

**Caption on screen:** The mast cell releases histamine; at H1 receptors it causes itch, edema, redness and watery eyes (slides 48–49, 52; 11/17 T@41705).

**Narration (112 words):** In the second step the mast cell degranulates. The granules of histamine move out toward the H one receptors on the eye tissue. Histamine is a central player in immediate hypersensitivity and allergic responses. When histamine binds H one receptors in the eye, it causes itching, swelling, called edema, redness and watery eyes. It also relaxes small blood vessels, so the vessel in the picture is drawn wider. These are the symptoms the drugs must control. There are two ways to do that. One is to keep histamine from acting on the receptor. The other is to keep the mast cell from releasing it. The next step shows drugs that do both.

**Sources:** Slides 48-49, 52; 11/17 T@41462-@43500

### Step 3 of 4

**Caption on screen:** H1 inverse agonists block the receptor; most are also mast cell stabilizers, so less histamine is released (slides 51, 53–54; 11/17 T@44333).

**Narration (117 words):** The third step adds ophthalmic H one inverse agonists, which people call antihistamines. The green blocks on the receptors are the drug. An inverse agonist reduces the constitutive activity of the receptor, its activity without histamine, and competes with histamine for binding. Most of these drugs also stabilize mast cells, shown by the dashed ring, so less histamine is released. Dr. Smith said how this works is unclear. Cetirizine is the one without mast cell stabilization. Stabilizers are used before the allergen arrives, for example before cedar pollen season, because itch is harder to stop after histamine is released. Alcaftadine also blocks H four receptors, and epinastine also blocks H two receptors, which may reduce eyelid edema.

**Sources:** Slides 51, 53-54; 11/17 T@44333-@47500, T@51500-@52000

### Step 4 of 4

**Caption on screen:** α2 agonists act on the blood vessels: vasoconstriction reduces the redness and the leak behind the edema (slides 56–57, 59; 11/17 T@52404).

**Narration (120 words):** The fourth step adds a second class, alpha two agonists. They do not act on histamine. They stimulate adrenergic receptors on the blood vessels, shown by the green bar, and cause vasoconstriction, a narrowing of the vessels. That reduces redness, and it reduces the leaking of fluid from capillaries that contributes to edema. The list is naphazoline, oxymetazoline, tetrahydrozoline, brimonidine tartrate and phenylephrine. Dr. Smith's list groups phenylephrine with the alpha two agonists, although other references call it an alpha one agonist. For itchy eyes, Dr. Smith said there is not much difference between these drugs. Naphazoline with pheniramine combines an alpha two agonist and an antihistamine in one drop. So antihistamines treat itch, and vasoconstrictors treat redness and edema.

**Sources:** Slides 56-57, 59; 11/17 T@52404-@54200

## Dry eye: where each drug acts (ey-dryeye)

**What it explains.** How inflammation lowers tear production in dry eye (keratoconjunctivitis sicca) and where lifitegrast, cyclosporine, loteprednol and varenicline act. Dr. Smith, 11/17.

### Step 1 of 4

**Caption on screen:** Dry eye (keratoconjunctivitis sicca): inflammation in the lacrimal system lowers tear production (slides 62, 64; 11/17 T@57918).

**Narration (121 words):** This figure explains dry eye, also called keratoconjunctivitis sicca, and where each dry eye drug acts. It comes from Dr. Smith's lecture on dry eye. At the top left are the lacrimal glands and ducts, which make tears. The tears form the basal tear film over the eye. The tear film has three layers, lipid, aqueous and mucin, drawn at the bottom above the cornea. Dr. Smith teaches that the pathology is inflammation within the lacrimal system, in the glands or along the ducts, and that this inflammation reduces the eye's ability to make tears. Tear production falls, and that is dry eye. Risk factors are older age and female sex. Antihistamines, decongestants, antidepressants and hormone replacement therapy can cause it.

**Sources:** Slides 5-6, 62, 64; 11/17 T@56000-@58500

### Step 2 of 4

**Caption on screen:** Lifitegrast: LFA-1 on leukocytes binds ICAM-1; blocking it may inhibit T-cell adhesion and cytokine secretion (slide 71; 11/17 T@64155).

**Narration (126 words):** The second step shows the inflammation as a T cell, a type of leukocyte. On its surface is L F A one, lymphocyte function-associated antigen one, a protein found on leukocytes. L F A one binds I C A M one, a cell adhesion molecule. These molecules help white blood cells move to and stay at a place of inflammation, where they secrete inflammatory cytokines. Lifitegrast, one drop in each eye twice daily, binds L F A one and blocks that interaction, shown by the red bar. Dr. Smith said this may inhibit T cell adhesion and the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, so inflammation falls indirectly. Patients remove contact lenses before the drop and reinsert them fifteen minutes later, because the drug may discolor the lenses.

**Sources:** Slide 71; 11/17 T@64155-@66500

### Step 3 of 4

**Caption on screen:** Cyclosporine binds cyclophilin; the complex inhibits calcineurin, so less IL-2; loteprednol is a corticosteroid (slides 65–67, 69; 11/17 T@60561).

**Narration (117 words):** The third step shows the signalling inside the T cell that cyclosporine blocks. Cyclosporine, in the green box, binds a protein called cyclophilin. The complex inhibits calcineurin, the enzyme that normally allows N F A T to express interleukin two, I L two, which makes T cells multiply. So less I L two is made, inflammation falls, and tear production increases. Dr. Smith places calcineurin in the signal cascade, after signals one and two. Cyclosporine is used as an eye drop, not by mouth, because oral cyclosporine is a significant immunosuppressant and raises the risk of opportunistic infections. Loteprednol is a corticosteroid eye drop used for its anti-inflammatory effect, because corticosteroids lower the expression of inflammatory genes.

**Sources:** Slides 65-67, 69; 11/17 T@59500-@64155

### Step 4 of 4

**Caption on screen:** Varenicline: nasal nicotinic receptors activate the trigeminal parasympathetic pathway: more basal tear film (slide 73; 11/17 T@67641).

**Narration (109 words):** The last step shows a different mechanism. Varenicline is a nicotinic acetylcholine receptor agonist, first used for smoking cessation. For dry eye it is a nasal spray, one spray in each nostril twice daily, about twelve hours apart. In the figure the nasal cavity holds the nicotinic receptors. Binding there activates the trigeminal nerve, which activates a parasympathetic pathway, the dashed line running to the lacrimal glands. This increases production of the basal tear film, and the tear layers are drawn thicker. Rare or infrequent side effects include acquired night blindness and blurred vision. It is highlighted here, with its transporters drawn as an arrow going in and an arrow coming out, and its enzymes drawn as small circles. It has influx and efflux transporters, proteins that carry drugs in and out, and enzymes that include esterase and C Y P enzymes. Its surface is larger, so more drug enters. Doctor Sikazwe called the conjunctiva the primary route for large, hydrophilic drugs, which dissolve well in water. Hydrophilic drugs therefore enter by this route.

Sources: Sikazwe slides 29, 30; 11/18 T@44206

### Step 4
Caption on screen: Both routes lead to the drug's ocular bioavailability; the conjunctiva's enzymes can activate a prodrug (slide 30; T@44422, T@45075).

Narration (97 words): In this step both routes lead to the aqueous humor, the fluid inside the front of the eye, and then to the drug's ocular bioavailability, the fraction that is actually available in the eye. The cornea is the primary route for small, lipophilic drugs. The conjunctiva is the route for large, hydrophilic drugs, and its enzymes can activate a prodrug, an inactive form that is converted to the active drug in the body. This is why some drugs are given to the eye as prodrugs. So the route can be predicted from the drug's lipophilicity and size.

Sources: Sikazwe slide 30; 11/18 T@44422, T@45075

## oo-imidazoline: Imidazoline alpha-agonists: SAR

### Step 2
Caption on screen: With a CH2 linker most agents are alpha-1 agonists; with an NH linker most are alpha-2 agonists (slide 32; T@50385).

Narration (104 words): The linker atom decides the receptor. Doctor Sikazwe said that when the linker is a carbon, most of those agents are alpha one receptor agonists. When the linker is an amine, most are alpha two selective. The slide prints X equals C H two for alpha one agonists and X equals N H two for alpha two agonists. The figure draws N H, which is the linker in the three drugs on the previous slide. Both statements say most, not all. For glaucoma the amine linker matters, because the drugs wanted here are alpha two agonists. The next three steps show the three drugs.

Sources: Sikazwe slide 32 (slide 32 is outside the assigned pages); 11/18 T@50385. Slide prints X=NH2; spoken: amine

### Step 3
Caption on screen: Clonidine, the prototype (ClogP 1.5), reaches the brain and brings CNS effects (slide 31; T@46183, T@46562).

Narration (100 words): Clonidine is the prototype, the reference molecule from which the other drugs were inspired. The figure draws the aryl ring with a chlorine on each side of the linker, an N H linker and the imidazoline ring, and a scale of C log P, a measure of lipophilicity. Clonidine has a C log P of one point five. Doctor Sikazwe said that is lipophilic enough to get into the brain, where it brings several unwanted effects on the central nervous system, including hypotension. The goal is to treat glaucoma, not to lower blood pressure, so this makes clonidine less desirable.

Sources: Sikazwe slide 31 (ClogP 1.5); 11/18 T@46183, T@46562

### Step 4
Caption on screen: Apraclonidine adds an amine: ClogP 0.87, less lipophilic, less brain entry (slide 31; T@47174, T@47684).

Narration (89 words): Apraclonidine is the amine analog of clonidine, drawn with an added H two N group on the ring. Its C log P is zero point eight seven, lower than clonidine's, which means it is less lipophilic. Because it is less lipophilic it does not get into the brain well, so the worry about central nervous system effects, hypotension among them, is reduced. Doctor Sikazwe gave its half-life as about eight hours, so it is useful and fairly long lasting. The next drug was made to improve on it further.

Sources: Sikazwe slide 31 (ClogP 0.87, Iopidine); 11/18 T@47174, T@47684 (transcript garbles the name; slide prints apraclonidine)

### Step 5
Caption on screen: Brimonidine, the pyrazine analog (ClogP 1.2), is the most potent and highly alpha-2 selective (slide 31; T@47934, T@48347).

Narration (97 words): Brimonidine is the pyrazine analog, drawn with a pyrazine ring fused to the aryl ring and a bromine on the ring. Its C log P is one point two. Doctor Sikazwe called it the most potent of the series and highly selective for alpha two, about a thousandfold more potent than apraclonidine and clonidine. A small drop in the eye is enough, with less worry about entry into the brain. Its half-life is three to four hours, shorter than apraclonidine's, so it is instilled more frequently. This is why many products contain brimonidine as the active ingredient.

## oo-ache: AChE inhibitors: ester, carbamate, phosphoryl

Explains: Ranking of AChE inhibitors by functional group.

### Step 1
Caption on screen: Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) hydrolyzes ACh, an ester, to acetic acid and choline, inactivating it (slide 41).

Narration (99 words): This figure explains how drugs that inhibit acetylcholinesterase, called A C h E, are ranked by functional group. It comes from Doctor Sikazwe's lecture on glaucoma, in the part on cholinergic agents. Acetylcholine, called A C h, is an ester. The enzyme A C h E hydrolyzes it, which means it splits it with water, into acetic acid and choline, and in doing so inactivates it. The drawing shows acetylcholine on the left, an arrow labelled A C h E, and acetic acid and choline on the right. The next step shows what happens when this enzyme is blocked.

Sources: Sikazwe slide 41; 11/19 T@17300-@18000 (hydrolysis of ACh; choline recycled)

### Step 2
Caption on screen: Indirect-acting cholinergics inhibit AChE, so ACh levels rise and fluid outflow increases (slide 41).

Narration (89 words): Indirect-acting cholinergics are drugs that act on this enzyme instead of on the receptor. They inhibit acetylcholinesterase, drawn as a cross over the arrow. With the enzyme blocked, less acetylcholine is broken down, so acetylcholine levels rise. More acetylcholine then activates the cholinergic receptors, and fluid outflow increases, which is the effect the slide gives for these drugs. The slide states the mechanism as inhibiting the enzyme and thereby increasing acetylcholine levels. How strongly a drug inhibits the enzyme depends on its functional group, as the next step shows.

Sources: Sikazwe slide 41; 11/19 T@17300-@18400

### Step 3
Caption on screen: The extent of inhibition depends on the group: phosphoryl > carbamate > ester, so the carbamate is intermediate (slides 42-43).

Narration (94 words): The slide states that the extent of acetylcholinesterase inhibition depends on the functional group present. The ranking is phosphoryl, then carbamate, then ester, each greater than the next. The phosphoryl group is the most, the carbamate is intermediate and the ester is the least. Doctor Sikazwe described the same order as duration of action: phosphoryl longest, carbamate intermediate, ester shortest. He also placed an amide between the carbamate and the ester as mild, but the slide does not show an amide. So the answer to what functional group gives intermediate inhibition is the carbamate.

Sources: Sikazwe slides 42, 43 (Question 2); 11/19 T@21200-@22600 (spoken as duration of action; amide placed mild)

### Step 4
Caption on screen: Echothiophate's phosphoryl group stays on AChE; aging (loss of ethanol) leaves it irreversibly inactive (slides 42, 68).

Narration (89 words): The last step shows echothiophate iodide, an organophosphate, which the slide calls an irreversible acetylcholinesterase inhibitor. On the left, a phosphoryl group, drawn with two ethoxy groups, is attached to the enzyme, which is inactive. With water, the complex undergoes aging, which means one ethyl group is lost as ethanol, leaving a P O H group. The enzyme is now irreversibly inactive. This is why the phosphoryl group is the answer to which functional group gives irreversible inhibition. It also matches the longest duration of action in the ranking.

Sources: Sikazwe slide 42 (echothiophate, aging scheme), slide 68 (Question 5; outside the assigned pages 33-48); 11/19 T@18900-@20400 (aging = O-deethylation)

## oo-pg-prodrug: Prostaglandin FP agonists: ester prodrugs

Explains: Why most FP agonists are ester prodrugs.

### Step 1
Caption on screen: PGF2a carries a carboxylic acid at C-1 and an OH at C-15; FP agonists raise fluid outflow (slides 44, 46).

Narration (123 words): This figure explains how the prostaglandin drugs used in glaucoma are built, and why most of them are prodrugs. It comes from Doctor Sikazwe's lecture on glaucoma, in the part on prostaglandin receptor agonists. The slide states that all of them increase fluid outflow by activating the prostaglandin F receptor, also called the F P receptor. The drawing is the natural prostaglandin, P G F two alpha, simplified: a five carbon ring with two hydroxyl groups, one chain ending at carbon one as a carboxylic acid, and another chain carrying a hydroxyl group at carbon fifteen. Carbon one and carbon fifteen are the places that matter in this figure. A prodrug is an inactive drug that the body converts to the active one.

Sources: Sikazwe slides 44, 46; 11/19 T@22500-@23300 (natural PGF2 alpha too unstable, so analogs)

### Step 2
Caption on screen: Latanoprost carries an isopropyl ester at C-1, so it is a prodrug (slides 44-46).

Narration (93 words): Latanoprost carries an isopropyl ester at carbon one, in place of the acid, so it is a prodrug. In the figure the acid has become C O O isopropyl. The slide lists travoprost and tafluprost as isopropyl esters as well, and latanoprostene bunod is also an ester. Bimatoprost is different: it is an ethyl amide, and amides are active as they are. Esters, in general, are prodrugs. Doctor Sikazwe asked students to note this: the esters are prodrugs and the amides are active. The next step shows how an ester prodrug becomes active.

Sources: Sikazwe slides 44, 45, 46; 11/19 T@23000-@23800

### Step 3
Caption on screen: An esterase hydrolyzes the ester to latanoprost acid, the active drug (slide 45; esterase, slide 29).

Narration (92 words): An esterase, an enzyme that hydrolyzes esters, splits the isopropyl ester back to a carboxylic acid. In the figure, the carbon one end changes from the ester to a C O O H group, and the drug is now labelled latanoprost acid, the active form. The slide labels latanoprost as the prodrug and latanoprost acid as active, with esterase as the arrow between them. Doctor Sikazwe called this hydrolytic bioactivation. The conjunctiva is a place where this can happen, because its enzymes include esterase, as the figure on eye drug entry shows.

Sources: Sikazwe slides 45, 29; 11/19 T@23800-@24300 (carboxylesterase, hydrolytic bioactivation)

### Step 4
Caption on screen: Acids and amides are active and esters are prodrugs; C-15 OH or halogens prevent 15-OH oxidation (slides 46-48).

Narration (102 words): The structure activity relationship has three points. Acids, with a C O O H group, and amides, with a C O N H group, are active drugs. Esters are prodrugs. At carbon fifteen, the slide lists an O H or halogens, which prevent oxidation of the fifteen hydroxyl group. Doctor Sikazwe explained that replacing the hydroxyl with a halogen stops the oxidation to a ketone and stabilizes the molecule; the drawing shows tafluprost with two fluorines there. Two newer drugs follow the same ester prodrug rule and need esterase: omidenepag isopropyl, an E P two agonist, and netarsudil, a rho kinase inhibitor.

Sources: Sikazwe slides 44, 46, 47, 48; 11/19 T@24700-@29500 (halogen stops oxidation to ketone; omidenepag EP2; netarsudil isoquinoline, ester prodrug)
